## How

> There are two strategies to cutting the time short, like **the tortoise and the hare.**
>
> The **hare moves as fast as possible**, hands‑a‑blur (...). The **tortoise proceeds** ***deliberately***, with no wasted movements, measuring twice, cutting once (...). Everything proceeds in orderly fashion. 
>
>If the hare makes too many minor missteps and has to keep adjusting, the tortoise wins. If the tortoise spends too much time planning each step, the hare wins.” 
>
> *'When breaths becomes air' - Paul Kalanithi*

When I decided to learn programming(about 2 years ago) I've started with [The Complete Full-Stack Web Development Bootcamp](https://www.udemy.com/course/the-complete-web-development-bootcamp/) By Ms. Angela Yu. \
Back then I had no clue even what **an IDE** was, and the course had like a million five-star reviews so I basically took Ms. Angela words *for granted*.

Some of her word clearly went over my head, but some I've internalized. One of the things I've took for common truth was:
> Often, these error messages may seem like they are written by aliens because they do not make immediate sense. However, you can usually understand their meaning by **searching the error message in Google**, which will provide many answers about the cause and how to fix the problem.

Seems like a good advice right? I mean that's what *everybody* does. Well I don't think it's right for me. \
Errors are often caused mostly by a fault in logic or a typo, so my natural reaction should be to **read the error message**, check the code and **the documentation**. **Google should be used as a last-hope**.
But that's not what I did. I wanted the solution *as quick as possible* - right now.
As if how fast I could remove the error message ment how good of a programmer I was.
I scanned the forum posts copy-pasting solutions, many times without even reading the explanation.
And when the snippet *clicked*, I didn't stop to contemplate the problem, just went on writing more code. **That's no way to learn**.

How did I come about this? Well I've decided to install **[Arch](./Arch.md)** lately. And I decided that I want to do it myself, *fully*. I relied pretty much only on the **wiki**. This made me not only better understand **the problems** that arose but also **the system**. I've also got much more comfortable with using documentation, and it gave me much more satisfaction.

Abusing Google and stack overflow you learn and build like **a hare**. You shoot all around the place, trying solutions from diffrent post till one will turn out to be the one. \
On the other hand analyizing the stack trace, code and documentation you learn and solve like a **turtle**, with no random movements, slowly building understanding of the problem.

I believe that **the turtle** approach is the most valuable, for it **deepens understanding** is driven by intendion and care for the craft.
## From whom 

### Background
Wondering around the library today, one book took my attention. \
It was the **"Introduction to Programming in Java: An Interdisciplinary Approach" by Robert Sedgewick**. \
Huge around 800 pages long book. Looking at it, made me imagine going through it. 

I wondered about ***how many great insights*** are in there to get from such an experienced teacher and scientist? 
Even though I'm much beyond the introductory courses to programming,  how much insight could i gather? 

But then I though about how long it would take. How *artificial* would solving these little example problems feel.
How again, **I would be learning not from the feedback from the real world but from the comments of a teacher** 

And then i thought about **the alternative**. About **building projects that interest me**. About **creating and validating my knowledge as I go as opposed to amassing facts that I don't use, of importance that I don't get.**

### Thesis 
Two types of learning rely on these principles:
1. **Learning theory is a prerequisite to practice**
  - Memorizing and following a teacher. Trusting him on authority. Preparing yourself thoroughly before facing actual tasks.
2. **Learning theory is to supplement and improve practice**
  - Focus on building and learn theory on a need-to basis, validating different approaches by the collision with the real world with disregard to authority.
